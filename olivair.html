OlivAir

A reactor that uses CO₂ dissolved in water as the acid to dissolve calcium-rich waste such as steel slag, and controls how long each grain stays in it. Modeled so far, not yet built. Parts are covered by a filed U.S. provisional patent application.

What OlivAir does

Steel plants make CO₂ and calcium-rich waste at the same site. OlivAir uses one to treat the other.

1

The CO₂ is the acid

Exhaust CO₂ dissolves in water as carbonic acid. A strong acid would dissolve the same calcium but leave a neutral salt, so no CO₂ would be stored.

2

The acid dissolves the calcium

It pulls calcium out of slag or kiln dust. Each calcium ion leaves carrying two CO₂ as two bicarbonate ions.

3

The CO₂ stays out of the air

The bicarbonate leaves as dissolved alkalinity, or is precipitated as solid carbonate, depending on the route.

What is new

The vessel

The chemistry is published (Shank et al., 2026; Ragipani et al., 2019). The problem is time: each grain needs a set time in the acid, and in an ordinary stirred tank every grain gets a different one.

The bench tests that matter most are the dissolution rate of real slag in carbonic acid, and whether partly dissolved grains really do become lighter and rise.

What it is not

Storage, not air removal.

The CO₂ comes from an exhaust stack, so this stores captured point-source CO₂. It does not remove CO₂ from the air, and I do not claim removal credits.

Where it stands

TRL 2 of 9

Analytically modeled, not yet built or tested. With no credit applied, the model puts the cost at about $175 to $560 per net tonne without a favorable host. That is above the $85 US tax credit for point-source capture, if the project even qualified. One steel-mill case comes in under it, on two prices that have no source.

The mark

Three things had to be true at once for this mark to work — watch, or click through them.

The recycling symbol is the most recognized environmental icon in the world — circulation, reuse, continuity. The OlivAir mark starts with that familiarity and breaks it: three arms begin exactly as a loop would, then bend inward instead of closing it. Three streams enter the system: exhaust CO₂, calcium-rich solids, and water. What looks like circulation is termination — the CO₂ is meant to stay locked away and not return to the air.

Where the loop would close, the mark narrows to a downward point instead — the reactor, where CO₂, water, and mineral converge. The CO₂ leaves as dissolved bicarbonate alkalinity or as solid carbonate, depending on the route, in forms meant to stay out of the atmosphere on a human timescale.

In the dark space the converging arms leave behind, a plant form emerges — not added as decoration, but carved by the geometry itself. I didn't invent the reaction. I just stopped wasting it.

The reactor

One vertical vessel with four functional zones. Parts of it are covered by a filed U.S. provisional patent application (No. 64/124,248).

Platform architecture

OlivAir reactor

Acid enters at the base, so the pH is lowest at the bottom and rises toward the top, and each zone has its own residence time.

Patent filed

Hydrodynamic classification chamber

Zone 0 holds coarse, unreacted grains low and carries partly dissolved fines upward to the impeller, so each grain gets the time it needs in the acid.

Patent filed

Zone 0 floor and wall acid delivery

A floor manifold pointing straight up and five rows of wall nozzles angled up and inward combine into one net upward velocity across Zone 0.

Platform architecture

Hollow baffle

Two walls of different length joined by a slanted, louvered front face, mounted against the vessel wall. The liquid circling the vessel has to meet the shorter Wall 2 first. The baffles are meant to catch fines and precipitating metals.

Questions, answered
What is OlivAir?+
An independent research project to design a reactor for industrial CO₂ mineralization. It uses CO₂ dissolved in water as the acid to dissolve calcium-rich solids such as slag, and the CO₂ ends up as dissolved bicarbonate or solid carbonate. It stores CO₂ from point sources; it does not remove CO₂ from the air. It is modeled, not built.
Are you looking for research opportunities?+
Yes — I'm always open to conversations about carbon removal, industrial chemistry, or reactor engineering research.
Are the cost figures measured?+
No. They come from a desk model. Inputs are cited or flagged as assumptions, and bench measurements are still to come.
Do you build as well as write the chemistry?+
So far OlivAir is analytically modeled (TRL 2), not yet built. I've laid out a lab-scale experimental roadmap and that's the next step.
What's your availability?+

Momentum
2023
Frost Science STEM Challenge — 2nd place
The first version of OlivAir was a passive, self-powered olivine-weathering device: solar-driven convective airflow, a photocatalytic TiO₂/zeolite coating, and an electret-based moisture system, with no electrochemistry or industrial input. It placed 2nd in a team engineering-design competition on climate change hosted by the Phillip and Patricia Frost Museum of Science in Miami.
2023 – 2026
Platform paper and application extensions
The project was reworked from a passive weathering device into an industrial reactor, first written up as a platform paper and ten application extensions. The design has changed several times since, and the current vessel uses CO₂ dissolved in water as the acid. The current version is in a November 2026 design overview.
2026
Steel-slag iteration, shared with Yale researchers
A later steel-slag version led to early-stage correspondence with researchers at Yale University, sharing the platform paper as it developed.
Aug 2, 2026

Techno-economic model
Built a desk cost model of 131 scenarios with sourced inputs, flagged assumptions, and a verification log. It is a model, not a measurement: the next step is bench data to test it.
Ongoing
Advised by Dr. Mike McCormick (Hamilton Biology & Environmental Engineering)
Weekly meetings since mid-September 2026. Lab safety training is complete; bench work has not started. The first planned experiment replicates Montserrat et al. (2017)'s olivine dissolution study, testing whether a siderophore-producing microbe closes the dissolution gap that study found.
