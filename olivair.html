OlivAir

A study of how steel-slag fines can be put to work: stabilized with stack-gas CO₂, and used in place of quicklime to neutralize acid mine drainage. Costed and modeled so far. Nothing is built and nothing is measured yet.

What OlivAir is now

Steel mills make slag fines that are hard to sell. Mines make acid water that is costly to treat. OlivAir asks whether one can help with the other.

1

Fresh fines swell

Steel slag carries free lime, which swells when wet, so the finest sizes cannot go into roads or concrete as they are. They are aged in the yard for months, or landfilled.

2

Stack gas can age them fast

In a covered bay, treated stack gas rises through 1 to 4 mm fines. The free lime turns into calcium carbonate, the fines stop swelling, and a little CO₂ is stored along the way. The chemistry follows Nippon Steel's published work (2008).

3

The finest fines neutralize acid

Fines under 1 mm are the most reactive. At a mine, a two-stage pulsed bed uses them in place of quicklime to bring acid water up to a neutral pH and drop out the iron.

What it is not

Storage, not air removal.

The CO₂ comes from a mill stack, so this stores a small amount of captured point-source CO₂. It does not remove CO₂ from the air, and I do not claim removal credits.

The mark

Three things had to be true at once for this mark to work — watch, or click through them.

The recycling symbol is the most recognized environmental icon in the world — circulation, reuse, continuity. The OlivAir mark starts with that familiarity and breaks it: three arms begin exactly as a loop would, then bend inward instead of closing it. Three streams enter the system: exhaust CO₂, calcium-rich solids, and water. What looks like circulation is termination — the CO₂ is meant to stay locked away and not return to the air.

Where the loop would close, the mark narrows to a downward point instead — the point where stack gas, water, and mineral converge. The CO₂ that is captured ends up in solid carbonate, in a form meant to stay out of the atmosphere on a human timescale.

In the dark space the converging arms leave behind, a plant form emerges — not added as decoration, but carved by the geometry itself. I didn't invent the reaction. I just stopped wasting it.

The three ideas

After many rounds of review the work narrowed to three modest ideas, each doing a known job more cheaply.

1. Fines lung

A static covered bay for 1 to 4 mm slag fines, fed chilled stack gas instead of months of yard ageing. About 15 to 20 kg of CO₂ is stored per tonne of fines. In the model the case rests on what the fines cost the processor today, not on carbon credits. Planned experiments: F5 (free lime and swelling in a covered bed) and a leach test.

2. Slag fines for acid mine drainage

Fine slag in place of quicklime, in a two-stage pulsed bed where the slag moves against the water, with aeration between the stages and a clarifier at the end. In the model the margin over lime is thin and depends on what the spent slag can be used for. Planned experiment: a bench test on real mine water.

3. A gas rule for dry-stack tailings (on hold)

Moving the gas through a stack of dry mine tailings only when the front breaks through, to cut fan energy. The model puts the ceiling near 58% less fan energy. On hold until the first two have data.

Questions, answered
What is OlivAir?+
An independent research project on what steel-slag fines can be used for: stabilizing them with stack-gas CO₂ so they stop swelling, and using them in place of quicklime to neutralize acid mine drainage. It stores a small amount of point-source CO₂; it does not remove CO₂ from the air. It is modeled, not built.

Yes — I'm always open to conversations about industrial chemistry, slag, mine water, or reactor engineering research.
Are the cost figures measured?+
No. They come from a desk model with placeholder prices that are flagged as such. Bench measurements and a vendor quote are still to come.
Do you build as well as model?+
So far OlivAir is modeled (TRL 2), not built. My own bench tests, starting with F5, come after the work I am doing with Dr. McCormick.

Momentum
2023
Frost Science STEM Challenge — 2nd place
The first version of OlivAir was a passive, self-powered olivine-weathering device: solar-driven convective airflow, a photocatalytic TiO₂/zeolite coating, and an electret-based moisture system, with no electrochemistry or industrial input. It placed 2nd in a team engineering-design competition on climate change hosted by the Phillip and Patricia Frost Museum of Science in Miami.
2023 – 2026
Platform paper and application extensions
The project was reworked from a passive weathering device into an industrial reactor, first written up as a platform paper and ten application extensions. The design changed several times, and after many rounds of review it narrowed in November 2026 to three modest ideas that use steel-slag fines.
2026
Steel-slag iteration, shared with Yale researchers
A later steel-slag version led to early-stage correspondence with researchers at Yale University, sharing the platform paper as it developed.
Nov 2026
Lean techno-economic model
Rebuilt the desk cost model as a lean workbook covering the three ideas, with placeholder prices flagged. It is a model, not a measurement: the next steps are a vendor quote and bench data.
Ongoing
Advised by Dr. Mike McCormick (Hamilton Biology & Environmental Engineering)
Weekly meetings since mid-September 2026. Lab safety training is complete; bench work has not started. My own experiments, starting with F5 (free lime and swelling of carbonated fines in a covered bed), come after the work Dr. McCormick sets.
