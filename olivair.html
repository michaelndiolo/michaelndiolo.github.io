OlivAir

OlivAir is not a company. It is the name I give to my study: three years of working on one question, whether one industry's waste can solve another industry's problem.

The three projects ↓ Where each stands →
The question

Steel mills make slag fines that are hard to sell. Mines make acid water that is costly to treat. I want to know whether one can help with the other.

Each project below is its own design with its own page. They share one material, steel-slag fines, and nothing else. Everything is modeled and costed on paper. Nothing is built, and nothing is measured yet.

Three projects
01
At the steel mill

The fines lung

Fresh slag fines swell when wet, so they cannot go into roads or concrete. This design ages them in days instead of months of yard ageing, and stores CO₂ while it does.

Against todayabout two days instead of 6+ months in the yard (target)
Model estimate: 15 to 20 kg of CO₂ stored per tonne of fines. Chemistry from Nippon Steel (2008). Planned: test F5.

02
At the mine

Slag fines for acid mine drainage

The same material, finer, used in place of quicklime to bring acid mine water up to a neutral pH and drop out the iron.

Against todayabout $117 a day cheaper than quicklime at a large site
Model estimate: pH 7 to 9 out, from acid water at pH 2 to 3. The margin over lime is thin. Planned: bench test on real mine water.

03
At the tailings stack

A gas rule for dry-stack tailings

Dry mine tailings can take up CO₂ from exhaust gas. This is a control rule for that process, aimed at cutting the fan energy per tonne of CO₂ stored.

Against todayup to 58% less fan energy than leaving the gas on (lab ceiling)
Model ceiling: about 58% less fan energy, from one lab test. Planned: test S1.

The mark

Three things had to be true at once for this mark to work — watch, or click through them.

The recycling symbol is the most recognized environmental icon in the world — circulation, reuse, continuity. The OlivAir mark starts with that familiarity and breaks it: three arms begin exactly as a loop would, then bend inward instead of closing it. Three streams enter the system: exhaust CO₂, calcium-rich solids, and water. What looks like circulation is termination — the CO₂ is meant to stay locked away and not return to the air.

Where the loop would close, the mark narrows to a downward point instead — the point where stack gas, water, and mineral converge. The CO₂ that is captured ends up in solid carbonate, in a form meant to stay out of the atmosphere on a human timescale.

In the dark space the converging arms leave behind, a plant form emerges — not added as decoration, but carved by the geometry itself. I didn't invent the reaction. I just stopped wasting it.

CaO + H₂O → Ca(OH)₂ Ca(OH)₂ + CO₂ → CaCO₃ + H₂O CaO + 2H⁺ → Ca²⁺ + H₂O

The chemistry is published. The design around it is what I work on.

Questions, answered
Is OlivAir a company?+
No. It is the name for my study, and the way I look at industrial waste. The logo is my own mark for that work. There is no company, no product and no customers.
What is the work, in one line?+
Finding a second use for steel-slag fines: stabilizing them with stack-gas CO₂ at the mill, and using the finest of them to neutralize acid mine drainage. It stores a small amount of point-source CO₂; it does not remove CO₂ from the air.
Why is some of the detail locked?+
The drawings, the step-by-step design and the 3D models are shared with people I am talking to. I give out one password, and it opens every locked section on the site at once. If you would like it, email me and tell me a little about why you are interested.
Are the numbers measured?+
No. They come from a desk model with placeholder prices that are flagged as such. Bench measurements and a vendor quote are still to come.
Do you build as well as model?+
So far it is modeled (TRL 2), not built. My own bench tests, starting with F5, come after the work I am doing with Dr. McCormick.
Are you looking for research opportunities?+
Yes. I am always open to conversations about industrial chemistry, slag, mine water, or reactor engineering research, alongside coursework and athletics at Hamilton.
Three years
2023
Frost Science STEM Challenge — 2nd place
The first version was a passive, self-powered olivine-weathering device: solar-driven airflow, a photocatalytic TiO₂/zeolite coating, and an electret-based moisture system. It placed 2nd in a team engineering-design competition on climate change at the Frost Museum of Science in Miami.
2023 – 2026
From a device to an industrial design
The study moved from a passive device to industrial reactor ideas, written up as a platform paper and ten application extensions. The design changed many times, and after many rounds of review it narrowed in November 2026 to three modest ideas that use steel-slag fines.
2026
Shared with Yale researchers
A steel-slag version led to early-stage correspondence with researchers at Yale University, sharing the platform paper as it developed.
Nov 2026
Lean cost model
Rebuilt the desk cost model as a lean workbook for the three ideas, with placeholder prices flagged. It is a model, not a measurement: the next steps are a vendor quote and bench data.
Ongoing
