Model and tests

What the cost model says, how far to trust it, and the tests that would settle what is open.

What the model is

The cost model is a workbook: 10 sheets, 131 scenarios, about 36,000 formulas. Each input is cited to a source or flagged as an assumption, and a verification log records what was checked against outside sources and what was not. It is a desk model. Nothing in it has been checked against bench data, and no equipment price has a vendor quote behind it.

The model prices one reactor design for 100 tonnes of CO₂ a day. Costs are per tonne of CO₂ stored. Gross counts everything the plant spends. Net also subtracts the CO₂ the plant emits through its own grid electricity, so it is usually the higher number. No credit is applied, so the cost per tonne is also the credit per tonne that would be needed to break even.

The reactor front end is the same in every case: grind the slag, leach it with CO₂, hold grains in Zone 0. The back end is a choice. Dissolve the alkalinity and discharge it to fresh water or the sea, or precipitate solid calcium carbonate on site.

What it assumes about the reactor
The gate is worth about $30 per tonne
With the gated Zone 0, the base case costs $304 gross per tonne. With a generic stirred tank in its place it costs $335 ($552 against $647 net). The gap comes from an assumption: that reacted grains get lighter and separate, with 5% leaving early. It is not a measurement.
ModeledUntested
Jets are not credited, and pre-dissolving costs
Dissolving the CO₂ in water before the nozzles adds about $31 gross per tonne in the base case ($305 to $335). Even if the jets doubled the calcium extracted, an assumption, the cost stays above the base at $323. So on the model’s terms the idea does not pay yet. It needs a bench result.
Expected, not measured
Zone 0 time and grain size matter less
Holding grains 1.5 to 6 hours moves gross cost by $17 to $45 per tonne. Grain size moves it by $7 to $94. That ranks them below the money inputs, and says nothing about whether the gate works.
Modeled
The discharge pH has a cost minimum
On the fresh-water route, cost is lowest near pH 6.75. Higher, the water must carry more calcium carbonate, so more water is needed. Lower, more dissolved CO₂ escapes at the outfall instead of being stored.

What decides the answer

The model ranks its inputs by how far each moves the answer. The largest are not about the reactor at all. They are the value of the leached residue (it swings the cost by $244 to $514 per tonne), the price of slag, the value of a carbonate product, and what a diffuser and outfall cost at a real site. None has a source yet.

Three measurements would settle most of what is open: a jar test of the effluent in real seawater, one host’s outfall design report, and a buyer’s quote for the carbonate and the residue.

Bench tests that would settle it

Each row is a question the model currently answers with an assumption.

Dissolution rate of real slag in carbonic acid
How long a grain needs, as a function of size and CO₂ pressure. The model anchors its rate to published studies and scales it with an assumption. This is the biggest single unknown for the reactor.

Do reacted grains become lighter and rise?
The gate depends on it. The expectation comes from the chemistry and has not been checked for slag.

How many grains leave early
The model assumes 5% of grains leave Zone 0 before they are finished. That figure is a placeholder.
ModeledUntested
Jet effect on dissolution
The size of the shear effect of the acid jets, and whether it repays the cost of pre-dissolving the CO₂.
Expected, not measured
Seawater jar test
Whether our effluent can be mixed into real seawater without precipitating, at the added-alkalinity cap the model assumes (500 µmol/kg).
Untested
Baffle catch and scaling
Whether the hollow baffles catch metals and fines, and whether they scale.
