Project 03 · at the tailings stack

The gas rule

Dry mine tailings can take up CO₂ from exhaust gas. A sensor at each block's outlet tells when it has had enough, so the gas moves on and the fan runs only while CO₂ is being taken up. Modeled on paper: nothing is built and nothing is measured.

The idea

A block of tailings takes up CO₂ fast at first, then very little. The fan should stop when it does.

How it works

Gas goes into one block

Exhaust, about 10% CO₂, is blown through buried pipes into one 2 m block of dry tailings. The bottom takes up CO₂ first and the reacted front rises.

A sensor watches the top

An outlet CO₂ sensor on each block reads about zero while the front is still inside, because almost all the gas is being stored.

Breakthrough

When the front reaches the top, CO₂ starts to come out and the reading climbs toward the inlet level. That is the signal that the block is nearly used up.

Move the gas on

At the trigger, the valve of that block closes and the next fresh block opens. The fan never pushes gas through tailings that are already full. The trigger value is set in test S1.

The lab figures are from FPX Nickel's Baptiste tests (0.5 kg of tailings, 10% CO₂, 14 days). How early breakthrough comes in a 2 m block is what test S1 measures.

What I changed

UBC's design injects exhaust into blocks of tailings and assumes all the CO₂ is stored. The rule: watch the outlet CO₂, and when it rises to match the inlet, move the gas to a fresh block, so the fan runs only while CO₂ is being taken up. It also measures the real capture instead of assuming it.

The catch

The 58% comes from one 14-day lab test on half a kilogram. In UBC's 6 m field pipe no CO₂ came out for 44 hours, so a field stack would save less, and each block stores only about 60% of its maximum. Test S1 is meant to find out how much.

Already known

Stopping at breakthrough is already published (Harrison, 2013). What I add is the energy ceiling for a dry stack.

What is mine, and what is next

What is mine

A control rule with a number.

The principle, that gas supply must not outrun what the tailings can take, is published (Harrison, 2013). What I add is the control: an outlet sensor on each block, the gas moved when capture falls, and the energy ceiling that follows. A second piece, cooling the gas so water does not wet the stack, matters only at warm sites or in warm seasons.
